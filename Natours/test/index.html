1. Introduction The domestic cat (Felis catus) is a small carnivorous mammal that has lived alongside humans for thousands of years. Today, cats are kept primarily as companion animals, but their relationship with humans began partly through their ability to control rodents around early agricultural settlements. Unlike many domesticated animals, cats retain many characteristics of their wild ancestors. They are skilled predators, highly sensory animals, territorial, and capable of living independently while also forming strong relationships with humans. Modern research increasingly treats cats as a distinct species with complex behavioral, nutritional, social, and welfare requirements rather than simply as "small dogs." 2. Scientific Classification Classification Cat Kingdom Animalia Phylum Chordata Class Mammalia Order Carnivora Family Felidae Genus Felis Species Felis catus Common names Domestic cat, house cat The domestic cat belongs to the Felidae family, which includes lions, tigers, leopards, cheetahs, and other felids. The domestic cat is closely related to the African/Near Eastern wildcat, particularly the lineage generally identified as Felis silvestris lybica. Genetic research strongly supports the Near East as an important center of cat domestication. 3. Origin and Domestication Cat domestication is particularly interesting because humans probably did not deliberately breed cats in the same way they bred dogs, cattle, or horses. The agricultural connection Approximately 12,000 years ago, humans in the Fertile Crescent increasingly adopted agriculture and began living in permanent settlements. Grain storage attracted rodents, and rodents attracted wildcats. This created a mutually beneficial relationship: Human settlements → stored grain → rodents → wildcats → fewer rodents Cats that were less fearful of humans could exploit this new food-rich environment. Humans, in turn, benefited from their hunting behavior. Researchers therefore describe early cat domestication as partly a commensal or mutually beneficial relationship. Archaeological and genetic evidence indicates that this relationship began in the Near East and subsequently spread. Ancient-DNA research has shown that both Near Eastern and Egyptian cat populations contributed to the ancestry of modern domestic cats. The worldwide spread of cats accelerated during later periods through human trade and migration. Ancient Egypt Cats became particularly important in ancient Egypt. They were associated with religious and cultural practices and were represented extensively in Egyptian art. However, modern research suggests that Egypt should not simply be described as the sole "birthplace" of the domestic cat. Instead, the evidence points toward an earlier Near Eastern origin followed by substantial development and expansion of cats in Egypt. 4. Physical Characteristics Cats are relatively small mammals, but their bodies are highly specialized for predation. Body structure Important physical adaptations include: Flexible spine Powerful hind limbs Retractable claws Sharp canine teeth Specialized carnassial teeth Sensitive whiskers Excellent hearing Strong sense of smell Highly developed visual system Flexible neck and shoulders Soft paw pads Long tail used partly for balance and communication Their skeleton and musculature allow them to accelerate rapidly, jump, climb, stalk prey, and make precise movements. Claws Cat claws are important hunting and climbing tools. Cats also use scratching to maintain their claws and communicate. Scratching can leave both visual marks and scent signals, making it a form of territorial communication rather than simply destructive behavior. 5. The Cat's Senses Cats experience their environment differently from humans. Vision Cats are adapted particularly well for detecting movement and functioning in relatively low-light conditions. Their eyes contain structures that increase the efficiency of available light. However, this does not mean cats can see in complete darkness. Cats also have a relatively wide field of view compared with humans, which is useful for detecting potential prey and threats. Hearing Hearing is one of the cat's most important senses. Cats can detect frequencies considerably higher than humans can hear. This ability is useful for detecting small prey, including rodents. The movable external ears, or pinnae, help cats determine the direction of sounds. Smell Smell plays a major role in: Identifying individuals Recognizing territory Detecting other animals Food selection Social communication Reproductive behavior Cats also possess the vomeronasal organ, sometimes called Jacobson's organ, which contributes to chemical sensing.
